009                                                                              A BSTRACT
010
011                  Expressive flow policies can represent multiple high-value actions, but sampling
012                  them with few integration steps while retaining tractable action densities remains
013                  challenging. We introduce DEFlow, a deep equilibrium flow policy that replaces
014                  explicit Euler sampling with implicit Euler integration and solves all intermediate
015                  actions jointly as an equilibrium system. We combine implicit differentiation with
016                  a discrete change-of-variables formula for the final-action density, enabling off-
017                  policy training within soft actor–critic without backpropagating through solver
018
                     iterations. DEFlow achieves an average relative improvement of approximately
                     10% in mean successful coverage over the strongest baseline across four Multi-
019
                     Goal docking variants. It also achieves the highest mean time-averaged return on
020
                     four of five manipulation tasks, while remaining broadly competitive across five
021                  MuJoCo tasks. These results support implicit flow policies for tasks requiring both
022                  diversity and precision, at the cost of additional nonlinear and linear solves.1

025   1        I NTRODUCTION
026
027   Soft actor–critic (SAC) is a strong baseline for continuous control. It combines data-efficient off-
028   policy learning with entropy-driven exploration (Haarnoja et al., 2018a;b). Its standard actor, how-
029   ever, is a single tanh-squashed diagonal Gaussian. This distribution is easy to sample from and has
030   a closed-form density, but it cannot easily represent several distinct high-value actions at the same
031
      state (Haarnoja et al., 2017). Diffusion and flow-matching policies offer a more expressive alter-
      native. They generate actions by transforming simple noise through multiple learned steps and can
032
      represent complex, multimodal distributions (Wang et al., 2023; Lipman et al., 2023). The challenge
033
      is to gain this expressivity while keeping SAC training practical. Sampling and actor differentiation
034   now involve a multi-step rollout, and the final-action log-density required by the entropy objective
035   may be hard to obtain.
036
037
      Existing off-policy methods resolve this tension by design compromise. Some give up the maximum-
      entropy objective altogether: Flow Q-Learning distills a multi-step generator into a one-step actor
038
      (Park et al., 2025), consistency policies replace the long sampler with one- or few-step generation
039
      (Ding & Jin, 2024), and Diffusion-QL combines diffusion behavior cloning with value maximiza-
040   tion without evaluating the policy likelihood (Wang et al., 2023). Q-score matching (QSM) (Psenka
041   et al., 2024) directly matches the policy score to the critic’s action-gradient and avoids backpropa-
042   gating through the complete sampler. SAC-Flow (Zhang et al., 2025), the direct parent of this work,
043   treats its explicit K-step flow rollout as a sequential model and stabilizes the recurrence with a
044   gated velocity field. DIME retains a multi-step denoising policy and trains its transitions through
045   approximate inference toward a Q-defined energy-based target (Celik et al., 2025). These methods
046   make expressive generative actors trainable off-policy, but leave a separate numerical question: how
047   should a multi-step policy be sampled when its learned velocity field becomes difficult to integrate
048
      with a small number of explicit steps? We investigate implicit-Euler sampling as an alternative.
049   We introduce DEFlow, a flow policy built around implicit-Euler sampling. We retain SAC-Flow’s
050   gated velocity field and multi-step actor, but evaluate each velocity at the unknown next action,
051   replacing the explicit Euler recurrence with implicit-Euler equations (Hairer & Wanner, 1996).
052   This integration rule is well suited to stiff dynamics, making it a natural alternative when a

090   2.2   F LOW AND D IFFUSION P OLICIES
091
      A flow policy is the expressive actor we adopt. Rather than a single Gaussian draw, it turns base
092
      noise z ∼ p0 into an action by transporting it through a learned velocity field. The state-conditioned
093
      transport follows ȧ(t) = vθ (a(t), s, t), starting from a(0) = z for t ∈ [0, 1]. A numerical integrator
094   turns this motion into T discrete steps; the endpoint is then squashed to keep the action bounded.
095   Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2023) provides a way to learn such fields from data. By transporting
096   samples from a simple base distribution, a flow can represent complex action distributions with
097   multiple distinct modes.
098
      Representing a richer action distribution, however, does not by itself make the policy easy to opti-
099
      mize with SAC. The standard actor update propagates gradients from the critic through the sampled
100   action to the policy parameters. Flow policies admit this differentiation, but direct optimization re-
101   quires propagating the learning signal through the entire sequence of sampling steps. This can be
102   computationally expensive and can lead to vanishing or exploding gradients, making stable policy
103   improvement a separate challenge from learning to reproduce an action distribution (Park et al.,
104   2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
105
      Our main comparisons concern expressive policies learned through online interaction. SAC-Flow
106   (Zhang et al., 2025), our direct predecessor, combines a gated velocity field with explicit Euler
107   integration and a surrogate entropy objective within SAC. DIME (Celik et al., 2025) instead uses a

      variational maximum-entropy bound, while Q-score matching (QSM) (Psenka et al., 2024) learns
109   the policy score from the critic’s action-gradient without evaluating a density.

      2.3    I MPLICIT M ODELS AND D IFFERENTIATION
112
113   Deep equilibrium (DEQ) models are implicit-layer networks (Bai et al., 2019): rather than stack
114   many explicit layers, a DEQ defines its output as the fixed point Z ⋆ = gθ (Z ⋆ ) of a single weight-
115   tied map and computes it with a root-finder. When gθ is a contraction, the iteration Z k+1 = gθ (Z k )
116   converges at a linear rate bounded by its Lipschitz constant. Anderson acceleration (Walker & Ni,
117   2011) uses a history of iterates and residuals to accelerate this solve.
118   The gradient follows from the solved equilibrium, without differentiating through the solver itera-
119   tions. For a continuously differentiable map with nonsingular I − ∂Z gθ (Z ⋆ ), implicit differentia-
120   tion turns the nonlinear forward problem into a linear system in the backward pass. Standard DEQ
121   implementations solve the adjoint system iteratively. Jacobian regularization can improve the con-
122   vergence of these solves (Bai et al., 2021). A complementary perspective treats backpropagation as
123   block-triangular back substitution (Edelman et al., 2024). We use the equilibrium formulation and
124   implicit differentiation to construct DEFlow in Section 4.

331   5.2   B ENCHMARK R ESULTS
332
333   Successful diversity under precision constraints. We extend the four-goal MultiGoal task
334   (Haarnoja et al., 2017) to test whether an expressive policy can combine diverse goal choices with
335
      precise actions. The agent receives a dense distance reward while navigating toward one of four
      symmetric goals. On entering a goal region, it performs one docking action and receives a bonus if
336
      the action is sufficiently close to the goal’s target. We study four variants combining wide or narrow
337
      goal regions with loose or tight docking tolerances. Wide goal regions are circles of radius 1, while

445   5.3                         A BLATION S TUDIES
446
447   Implicit vs. explicit flow. To test the role of implicit integration during policy learning, we re-
448   place DEFlow’s implicit-Euler updates with explicit Euler while retaining the actor architecture,
449   four integration steps, discrete density objective, and training settings. Both policies are trained in-
450   dependently on the docking tasks. DEFlow achieves higher successful coverage on loose docking
451
      (Table 1), and tight docking exposes a further limitation of the explicit variant: it either concentrates
      on a preferred goal or fails numerically (details in Appendix C.4). This comparison strongly supports
452
      implicit integration for learning diverse successful behavior under a narrow action tolerance.
453
454
      Integrator step-count ablation. On the MultiGoal variant with wide goal regions and tight dock-
455
      ing, we freeze two policies trained with T = 4 and change only the number of docking integration
456   steps. Each policy keeps its own integrator and uses the same states and Gaussian inputs across
457   step counts. At the training depth T = 4, the explicit policy achieves only 50.1% balanced docking
458   accuracy on a shared bank of reachable states, with near-zero accuracy for two of the four goals,
459   while DEFlow achieves 94.1%. Even on each policy’s own visited states, changing only the docking
460   integration depth from T = 4 to T = 2 reduces explicit success from 99.8% to 0.7%, versus 96.5%
461   to 53.7% for DEFlow, and increasing T also degrades the explicit policy. For this pair, DEFlow is
462   more robust to changes in step count (Figures 8 and 9).
463
464   6                        L IMITATIONS AND C OSTS
465
466   Training time. In our measured runs, DEFlow had roughly 40% lower effective training through-
467   put than SAC-Flow and took about three times as long as SAC. Its wall-clock cost was of the same
468   order as DIME’s. These comparisons are approximate and depend on workload and shared hard-
469   ware. Each implicit actor update requires a nonlinear forward solve and a dense linear backward
470   solve.
471
472   Exactness is tolerance-limited in training. Equation (13) is exact for a solution of the implicit
473   equations under the assumptions in Appendix A.3. The implementation uses finite solver tolerances
474
      and numerical modifications (Appendix C.1). The trained-policy audit in Appendix C.1 measures
      the resulting discrepancies at final manipulation checkpoints. The audit finds numerical inaccuracy,
475
      especially in PushWall actor gradients. Tightening the solver at evaluation reduces these errors, while
476
      sampled task success changes little. We use the original solver settings for benchmark evaluations
477   to avoid the extra cost of tighter solves.

482   We introduced DEFlow, a deep-equilibrium formulation of flow policies for maximum-entropy re-

      inforcement learning. By combining implicit sampling, tractable density evaluation, and implicit

      B.1   A RCHITECTURE
759
760   DEFlow uses the gated velocity field of SAC-Flow (Zhang et al., 2025). Its inputs are the obser-
761   vation, intermediate action, and time embedding. The time embedding maps 32 sinusoidal features
762   through two layers of widths 64 and 32. A shared projection with SiLU and layer normalization
763   feeds separate gate and candidate heads. Each head has one hidden layer and outputs an action-
764   dimensional vector. The gate is sigmoid, and the candidate is a scaled tanh as in Eq. (6). The velocity
765
      width is 256, the candidate scale is 4, and the gate output bias is initialized to 1. Actions start from
      standard Gaussian noise and use four implicit-Euler steps before the final tanh and action-bound
766
      scaling.
767
768
      Jacobian regularization. Motivated by Jacobian regularization for DEQ models (Bai et al., 2021),
769
      we penalize the individual velocity Jacobians:

774   and minimize Lπ + βRJ . We use σ = 0.9 and increase β from zero to 0.01 over the first 100,000
775   environment steps.
776
777   Temperature updates. In the regularized DEFlow recipe, we update log α from the actor log-
778   densities. The target is an exponential moving average of negative log-density (weight 0.01) minus
779   0.7, with floor −0.7da . The update winsorizes log-densities at the minibatch 5th and 95th per-
780   centiles, leaks log α toward log(0.05) with weight 0.01, and enforces α ≥ 0.02. We also tested
781
      fixed-temperature variants, which sometimes reached comparable return but did not provide stable
      adaptive temperature behavior.

1135
1136   Explicit flow. The docking audit illustrates the step-map conditioning effect identified in Sec. 3.
1137   In the notation of Eq. (4), the explicit-step Jacobian is Bk = Da Fkexp = I + ∆tJk . Three original
1138   explicit-flow runs terminated after detecting non-finite TD targets. At a frozen checkpoint from one
1139   of these runs, det(B0 + εI) changes sign between two sampled latent inputs. A float64 probe locates
1140   a singularity of this diagonally regularized matrix between them. At the same local J0 , the algebraic
1141   implicit counterpart I − ∆tJ0 has minimum singular value 1.226. The checkpoint thus exhibits the
1142
       singular explicit-step regime illustrated by the linear contraction in Appendix A.1.
1143
       QSM. QSM’s zero docking success is not a failure to reach the goal: 1,374 of 3,000 episodes
1144
       across three seeds reached the docking state, yet none docked. The failure lies in action generation.
1145
       At a frozen checkpoint, ideal goal-center observations produced action-box corners in 2,045 of 2,048
1146   samples. An exact replay of the five denoising steps shows that the final learned correction has
1147   median magnitude 16.0–21.6 in a box of [−1, 1]2 , pushing both coordinates outside the box in 99.4–
1148   100% of samples, so clipping maps the action to a corner. All values are finite and the final step adds
1149   no fresh noise. Why the correction is learned so large remains unresolved.
